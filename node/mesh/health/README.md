# Health and attachment

The engine owns one business WireGuard Device and one independent control Device. `NewAttachment` now builds the health-backed implementation; tests can still explicitly construct `newStaticAttachment`. Control traffic stays inside the data plane and does not enter the OS or the business ACL.

## Integration

- `healthConfig` in `../attachment.go` translates the applied config, including `nodes[].relays`, entry order, `fixed`/`failover`, `autoReturn`, and advanced health settings.
- `NewControl` registers data/control public keys with the managed transport, creates the control TUN and control Device. The control plane shares physical UDP/TCP/TLS paths with business traffic.
- Start the business Device before `Control.Start(manager)`. Stop the Manager before Control and the business Device. `Control.Close` does not close the shared business bind.
- `Manager` implements `Owner` and `ActiveEntry`; the wrapper implements the existing `mesh.Attachment`. Route changes call the engine's synchronous `reroute` hook outside the manager lock. Configuration changes are serialized separately from route changes.
- `Control` implements the internal `Backend`: Prepare, Send, Handshake, Warm, DataReady, Commit, Deactivate, Release, Remove. `Path` includes immutable ID and generation. Backend completion is not automatically protocol success.
- `Warm` never initiates a new data handshake on a trial when another active data path to the same peer exists. It uses an encrypted empty confirmation packet on that trial instead. For a new peer or recovery, explicit-path data handshakes are allowed. Both sides must have a completed data session before route-ready.
- Control packets require authenticated peer/path metadata, a matching control source/destination and mesh, nonce and path token/generation. The config revision is carried but not compared: the controller sends a new revision only to nodes whose config changed, so peers routinely run different revisions, and each node authorizes from its own applied config. Packet size, pending probes, control queues and per-peer inbound paths are bounded.

## Selection and ownership

The first candidate is the only candidate in fixed mode. Failover keeps an active path, one preferred available standby, and at most one temporary trial. Failed candidates back off; a newly detected hard outage grants one immediate rescue attempt without turning the normal cold scan into a busy retry loop. Initial preparation retains its ten-second handshake allowance.

Defaults are in `Defaults()`: active/standby 1/2 seconds, cold 30 seconds with backoff up to 300 (a dialed relay or direct link: up to `max(ColdRetry, 30 s)`, as traffic detours meanwhile), three consecutive deadline misses, three successful probes, adaptive timeout `clamp(4*SRTT+4*RTTVAR,2s,10s)`, six-second handshake recovery interval, sixty-second healthy return/residence, thirty-second lease with five-second refresh. Degradation needs two complete sixty-second windows. Queue expiry is not network loss; late replies do not rewrite an expired result.

Prepare/ready does not move business routes. The member durably allocates a sequence and sends commit; the new relay installs ownership and acknowledges; only then does the member change the active path/routes. Relays propagate notices and retry until acknowledgement or lease expiry. Local commit is not global convergence. Conflicting equal-sequence owners retain the last uncontested, unexpired route but cannot refresh it. Older sequence/refresh notices, and notices issued at a revision below the member's revocation high water, are rejected. The side that accepts a link answers probes with `reclaim` until it has seen `prepare` on that path, so after it restarts the dialing side prepares again and both sides commit the link.

`state.json.health` holds the sequence, accepted claims, config generation, authorized entry sets and revocation high waters. The engine's endpoint updates use the same serialized atomic state writer. Restored claims have no live monotonic lease. `health.initialized` detects loss of the state file and requires controller recovery instead of resetting the sequence.

## Punched links

A UDP path two nodes behind NAT open toward each other; `direct.go` owns it. Three kinds use the same machinery:

- a `links[]` entry `kind=direct` with `punch` between two members;
- a `links[]` entry `kind=relay` with `punch` between two relays (site relays without a public endpoint);
- an `entry.candidates[]` entry with `punch`: a member's entry through a site relay. The relay end needs no config of its own: it keeps state for a member allowed to attach to it once that member's offer arrives (at most 64, dropped after 10 idle minutes).

`dial` means nothing for a punched link; `endpoint`, if any (the other end's public UDP listener), is one more address to try. Without `self.listen.udp` the node keeps an ephemeral UDP socket (`transport.Options.UDP`) and offers its port.

- Discovery: the node asks `reflectors` in order, from the UDP socket WireGuard uses, which address it is seen from; every 60 seconds, after 5 seconds when no reflector answered, at once after a punched path is lost. Its offer is that address (`public:`) plus the machine's IPv4 interface addresses with its UDP port (`lan:`, at most six). A reachability round (below) updates the address too.
- Signals: control messages `offer` (addresses, resent until acknowledged as `session/gen`) and `punch` (an attempt's state, once a second while it lasts) travel over the authenticated control plane, never over the path being opened. A member sends them through its active entry, or another candidate that is up; a relay toward the addressee: to the relay a member is attached to, over a relay link, or through a linked relay that leads there (`nextHops`, then any linked relay). A relay forwards only what its authenticated sender names as `from` (or what a linked relay forwards), only between two ends that may punch (two members, two relays, or a member and one of its relays), at most three hops, and for each sender with a burst of 8 and 3 a second. An end takes signals only from a relay, and only for a link or candidate it punches (the site relay: for a member allowed to attach to it).
- Attempts: one end drives them, the lesser node ID of two members or relays, the member of an entry. It starts one once both ends have acknowledged each other's addresses, and numbers it; the other end follows the driver's `punch` messages. In attempt `n` the opener is the lesser ID for odd `n`, the other end for even `n`.
  - Both ends send business handshake initiations to the other's LAN addresses on one of their own interface networks and to its configured endpoint at once (`Backend.Punch`, through a `transport.PunchEndpoint` that carries only initiations and only until the attempt ends, 13.5 seconds).
  - The opener sends primes (`Backend.Prime`) to the other's `public:` addresses with IP TTL 4, 4, 3, 3, 6, 6 by attempt, repeating, every 250 ms for 1.5 seconds and every second after, and then says `opened`. The TTL is meant to run out between the two NATs: the prime opens the opener's NAT toward the other end and never reaches the other NAT. The opener sends the other's public addresses nothing else; it answers the handshakes that arrive, through the path they arrive on.
  - The other end adds the opener's public addresses to its handshakes only on `opened`, and with them the opener's LAN addresses off its own networks: such an address may be reached the long way round (a VPN between two sites), and sent with the public ones the first copy of an initiation to arrive, by the faster way, is the one that authenticates.
  - Why: a Linux NAT that takes a packet for a mapping its own host has not used yet keeps a flow for it, so the host's own packets to that peer leave from another port, and every further packet from the peer keeps that flow; two ends sending to each other's public addresses at once never meet.
  - Kinds of address (`via`): `lan` is an address the peer offered as `lan:` (or the configured endpoint) inside a network of one of this end's own interfaces (on-link); `configured` the configured endpoint otherwise; `public` one offered as `public:`, or any internet address; `routed` any other private address (10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16, 100.64/10, link-local), such as a LAN of the peer's reached through a VPN, or the router that masquerades for it. Only `lan` is preferred without measuring.
  - A UDP path of the peer to a `lan` address that authenticates business traffic ends the attempt at once. Otherwise every path that authenticates is pinged (control `ping` with `trial`, every 250 ms, at most 3 paths) until 3 seconds after the first did, or after the opener primed if later, and the one with the lowest median round trip is taken (`direct.chosen <peer> via <via> <addr>, <ms>`). Two paths within 1 ms, or a tenth of the faster one, are as fast: then `lan`, then `public`/`configured`, then `routed`. None answering, the first of the best kind. After a failed one (`direct.failed`, or `path.failed <candidate>: punch timeout` for an entry) neither end sends to the other's public addresses for 37 seconds (Linux keeps an unanswered UDP flow for 30), then retries back off from `ColdRetry` doubling to five minutes. A direct or relay link lost after it was up is tried again after a second.
  - Only the driver chooses: the other end takes no path by itself and waits, up to 8 seconds past the attempt, for the one the driver prepares (`direct.chosen <peer> via <via> <addr>, as <peer> chose`), then releases the others. A `trial` ping on a path the other end uses already is answered without touching that path's state.
  - A node offers every different public address its reflectors saw (at most 3, for a NAT that maps the port anew per destination), with LAN addresses cut to keep the offer within 8.
- Make before break (direct and relay links): the punched path is prepared and probed every `ActiveInterval`, which also keeps the NAT flows open. Routes go over it only once it is ready and route-ready on this end and the other says, in its pings and pongs (`direct`), that it is too. A direct link's peer gets its AllowedIPs; a relay link carries what `nextHops`, or the relay router, sends to that relay, and takes part in ownership notices, signal forwarding and routing like a dialed one (its probing path is `link:<peer>`). Either end seeing `ConsecutiveFailures` missed probes, the path gone or the other no longer ready takes the routes off first (`direct.down`), then releases the path.
- Faster paths (direct and relay links, so site relays' punched links too): the driver of a link that is up makes a probe attempt (`punch` with `probe`), which both ends run as above beside the link, leaving it up: every ten minutes for a `lan` link; otherwise after a minute, then two, four, eight and ten. A path other than the link's whose median over at least 3 answers is faster than the link's smoothed round trip by a fifth and by 5 ms is prepared and probed beside it (`direct.better`), committed when ready on both ends and then replaces it (`direct.switched`); if it is not ready within 20 seconds it is dropped and the link stays. The other end follows the path the driver prepares, also beside its link; the path a link left is released 30 seconds later, so the other end can still use it while it follows.
- Entry candidates: a punched candidate has no endpoint, and is not started, until a punched path to its relay authenticates; then the path is its endpoint and it is prepared, probed, switched to and back from like any other. When it fails, its endpoint is dropped and it punches again. An `endpoint` in a punched candidate's config (the controller fills in the relay's LAN address when both are on one network) is not its endpoint either: whenever the candidate has no path, no attempt runs and its retry is due, the member sends that address an initiation every 5 seconds, from start and with no relay needed to exchange addresses, and takes a path to it that authenticates at once. Attempts send to it too, and take it at once when it is on one of the member's networks, like a LAN address of the relay's.

Status: `peers[]` `endpoint`, `localAddr` and `remoteAddr` are the ends of the path in use, empty without one: `endpoint` as configured (a host name stays one) or where a peer's path comes from, `remoteAddr` its IP and port, `localAddr` this end's, for the UDP socket bound to every address the address the system sends from to `remoteAddr`. `peers[].direct {state, via, since, attempts, lastError}` for punched direct and relay links, `lastError` one of `no reflector`, `punch timeout`, `direct path lost`.

## Reachability

`reach.go`: every node, at start (once an entry or relay link is up, at most 20 seconds later), every ten minutes, when its interface addresses change and after a failed punch attempt (at most every two minutes), asks the reflectors and `reachability.stun` from the WireGuard socket and asks a relay to test its filtering. Mapping is `stable` when two or more observers saw one port, `varies` when they did not, `unknown` with one; `consistent` is false when they saw different IPs. While the public address and the interface addresses stay the same, a round that measures less keeps what earlier ones did: with one observer answering, the mapping last measured with two; without a test result, the filtering last measured; and `address`, when only the asked relay's test came through, does not take back an `open` (another relay may have skipped the address or been unable to send). A `port` result does.

The filtering test: the node asks a relay it is attached to, or for a relay one it is linked with, preferring one that is also a reflector, over the control plane (`reach`), and asks that relay's reflector first with the request's ID. The relay sends three 32-byte tests, from a socket of its own, to the address its reflector saw that ID come from, never one the node names, and asks up to two other linked relays to do the same (`reach-relay`), relays it dials first, as they are reachable from outside; it answers `reach-ack`. A relay asked by another skips an address it has heard from in the last five minutes (a reflector request or a UDP path), and tests one address at most every ten seconds. Requests are taken from nodes allowed here, three at once and then one per 20 seconds each; at most 32 wait, and 32 test sockets are open at once. The node asks the other observers only after the test. Two seconds after the ack: a test from another relay is `open`, only from the asked one `address`, none `port`; no answer is `unknown` and the next round is in two minutes. A node whose public address is one of its own and whose asked relay's test arrived is `open` too: no other relay may have been free to test it.

`verdict`: `public` for open filtering, `symmetric` for varying ports, `punchable` for stable mapping and `address`/`port` filtering, `no_udp` when no observer answered, `unknown` otherwise (inconsistent IPs, no test, no UDP socket), raised to `punchable` while a link punched through a `public:` address is up. Status `reachability {checkedAt, verdict, publicAddr, consistent, mapping, filtering, observers[{id, kind, addr, ok}]}`, a reflector named by the relay whose endpoint is on its host, or, for a relay that dials this one, whose UDP path comes from it; event `reach.changed` (`punchable -> symmetric`).

## Entry candidate status

`entry.candidates[] {id, relay, transport, state, since, lastOkAt, lastError, via, rttMs, lossRate, checkedAt, probes, timeouts, redial}` (`redial`: see Redial) for every candidate in use, standbys included, also while no entry is up. `state`: `active`, `ready` (prepared, route-ready, no missed probe), `failing` (the last attempt failed), `punching` (a punched candidate without a path yet), `untested`. `lastError`: `timeout` (dial, handshake or probes timed out, or the stream closed), `refused`, `tls pin mismatch`, `no handshake` (probes answered but the relay never became route-ready), `unreachable`, `punch timeout`. `via`, for a punched candidate with a path: `lan` (a LAN address of the relay's, or its configured endpoint on one of this node's networks), `public` or `configured`, as for direct links; empty otherwise. The last five are what the candidate's probes measured, kept across its paths (`meter.go`); a cold candidate's are those of its checks, which add no probe. `rttMs`: the median of the last five answers within the minute up to the last outcome, null without an answer there; `lossRate`: the share of the outcomes in that minute that timed out, null without one; `checkedAt`: the last outcome (ms), 0 before one; `probes`, `timeouts`: since the data plane started, monotonic under the candidate's ID, a failed attempt to connect or punch counting as one of each (`TestCandidateMeasurement`, netns `TestCandidateMeasurements` with `OBLAB_MEASURE=1`).

Round trips are stamped with `mesh/clock.Now`: a probe's write (transport), its answer's arrival (`control.go`) and the tick a trial ping goes out on. That is `time.Now` on Linux and macOS. On Windows `time.Now` moves only at clock interrupts (the interrupt time, 15.625 ms steps by default), so an answer within a step measured 0 and one across a step a whole step; `clock.Now` adds the performance counter there, its readings differing by what the counter measured, never going back, and staying within 62.5 ms (four steps) of `time.Now`: past that they run 1/32 slower or faster than the counter until back, rather than step or hold (`clock` `TestFineCounterOffRate`). An answer stamped within its probe's write counts from the write's start, and no answer measures less than 1 µs, so an answered probe always has a round trip (`TestAnswerWithinAClockStep`, `mesh` `TestCoarseClockStillMeasures`, netns `TestWindowsClockNetns` with `OBLAB_WINCLOCK=1`, a member on a simulated Windows clock on a 5.8 ms LAN).

## Redial

Some networks put each connection on one of several lines by its addresses and ports; a TCP connection that drew a lossy line stays slow for as long as it lasts, while a new one from another source port may draw a good one. `redial.go` therefore lets the end that dials a TCP or TLS path move it to a new connection when it stays degraded: relay links and configured (not punched) direct links this node dials, and a member's TCP/TLS entry candidates with a path. UDP and punched paths, and the accepting end, never redial.

Every minute of a path's probe outcomes (answers and timeouts) is judged once it holds 20 outcomes, 10 of them answers; a minute with fewer, as a cold candidate's, is not judged and breaks both runs. The baseline `b` is the least median of a judged minute within the last 6 hours, kept per hour (least median, minute count) and in `state.json` (`linkBaselines`, by candidate ID, `link:<peer>` for a link, dropped when the endpoint changes), once those hours hold 30 judged minutes. Thresholds: a slow answer is above `max(1.5b, b+30 ms)`; a bad minute has its median above that, or at least 10% of its outcomes slow or timed out; a healthy minute is not bad and has its median at most `max(1.25b, b+15 ms)`. Three bad minutes in a row redial. The backoff after a redial is 10, 20, 40, then 60 minutes; 10 healthy minutes in a row end it, and any two redials are 10 minutes apart at least. A failed redial counts as one. Skipped redials are no events.

The transport dials the new connection first (`transport.Bind.Redial`): the path keeps its ID and generation, sends go over the new connection once TCP, TLS with the pins and the preamble are up, and the old one still delivers until the other end answers over the new one without asking for it to be prepared again (then `Retire` closes it after a second) or 30 s pass. A new connection that authenticates nothing within 10 s while the old one is open hands the path back. While the other end prepares the new connection (it sees a new connection from the same peer), the routes stay on the path.

Events: `link.redial <id>[ relay=<peer>] reason=<rtt|slow> rtt=<median>ms baseline=<b>ms slow=<share>% port=<old>-><new>`, `link.redial.failed` with the same fields up to `slow=` and ` error=<text>` instead of `port=`. `reason=rtt`: the median was above the slow threshold; `slow`: 10% or more slow or timed out with the median below it. `<id>` is `link:<peer>` for a link, the candidate ID for an entry candidate (`relay=` its relay). Status: `peers[].redial` for links, `entry.candidates[].redial` for candidates, `{baselineMs, level, redials, lastAt, nextAt, degradedMinutes}`, absent for paths this node does not redial (`TestRedial*`, transport `TestRedial*`, netns `TestRedialNetns` with `OBLAB_REDIAL_NETNS=1` on test time).

## Relay routing

On relays, `routing.go` feeds the relay router (`../routing`, rules and parameters in its README.md) with every probe of a relay link, dialed or punched alike, probed every `ActiveInterval`; a link whose path goes away is reported down at once. Advertisements travel as `route-advert` messages between linked relays only, and a next hop change applies routes through the `Routes` hook.

## Status

The engine fills the existing peer `rttMs`/`timeoutRate`, entry candidate/switches/since, and owner seq/leaseMs fields. Endpoints are selected from actual committed paths rather than configured labels. Pending ownership acknowledgements are reported in the existing `error` field; configuration errors take precedence. Events include `link.redial`, `link.redial.failed` (see Redial), `direct.up`, `direct.down`, `direct.failed`, `reach.changed`, `path.ready`, `entry.prepare`, `entry.committed`, `entry.switch`, `entry.flapping`, `entry.unavailable`, `owner.changed`, `owner.ack`, `owner.unconfirmed`, and security/route errors. `path.ready <candidate>` and `path.failed <candidate>: <reason>` report a change of a candidate's outcome only: its first ready, a failure, ready again after one. A cold candidate's check every `ColdRetry` that finds it ready, or a retry failing with the reason of the one before, is no event (`TestColdChecksAreNoEvents`); `path.security` comes with a reported `path.failed` only. `direct.failed` likewise is not repeated for retries that fail as the one before until the link was up (`TestRepeatedPunchFailuresAreOneEvent`). Status keeps the current state of each candidate and link either way.

## Tests

```sh
cd node
export HOME=/root GOTOOLCHAIN=local GOCACHE=/tmp/go-build
go test -race ./mesh/health ./mesh
OBLAB_NETNS=1 OBLAB_CONTRACT=1 go test -race -run '^TestContract$' -timeout 3m ./mesh
OBLAB_HEALTH_ACCEPT=udp OBLAB_HEALTH_ROUNDS=30 go test -race -run '^TestHealthAcceptance$' -v -timeout 35m ./mesh
OBLAB_PUNCH=1 go test -run '^TestPunch$' -v -timeout 10m ./mesh
OBLAB_REACH=1 go test -run '^TestReachability$' -v -timeout 5m ./mesh
OBLAB_SITE=1 go test -run '^TestSiteRelays$' -v -timeout 5m ./mesh
OBLAB_NETNS=1 OBLAB_REDIAL_NETNS=1 go test -run '^TestRedialNetns$' -v -timeout 10m ./mesh
```

`TestLANEntry` (`oblab-le*`, `OBLAB_LANENTRY=1`): two members on a site relay's LAN enter it, punched; the one whose candidate has the relay's LAN address as its endpoint still does, over the LAN, after the public relay stops and it restarts, the other cannot. `TestPunch` puts a relay behind a 40 ms round trip and two members behind MASQUERADE routers in `oblab-p*` namespaces: `nat` (punched, business moves from TTL 63/80 ms to TTL 64), `symmetric` (`--random-fully`, stays relayed), `cut` (UDP between the NATs dropped and restored), `race` and `samenat` (both behind one router, LAN addresses). `race` uses plain Linux NATs, which keep a flow for an unsolicited packet, with five more hops between them, and runs three attempts the old way (`OBLAB_SIMULTANEOUS=1`: `health.SimultaneousPunch`, both ends at once), which must all fail, then three with opening, which must all get through; it logs the NATs' conntrack flows. `TestReachability` (`oblab-rq*`) judges `open`, `address`, `port`, `varies`, `no_udp` and inconsistent exits and checks a standby that times out. `TestReachMesh` (`oblab-rm*`, `OBLAB_REACHMESH=1`) rebuilds the mesh of revisions 26 to 28: a member becomes a site relay while running and both its reflectors answer, a public relay that site relays joined stays `public`/`open`, and a site relay whose UDP port changes while running gets its links back on the new socket. `TestPathChoice` (`oblab-pc*`, `OBLAB_PATHCHOICE=1`) joins two sites' LANs through a slow tunnel: the punched relay link takes the public path, and moves to the LAN path without going down once the tunnel is the faster way (`OBLAB_REPROBE` shortens the ten minutes). `TestSiteRelays` (`oblab-rp*`) has two site relays behind plain NATs punch their link, members enter them over LAN addresses, business between the homes goes over the punched link, and a member moves to the public relay when its site relay stops.

Acceptance modes: `udp`, `tcp`, `stop`, `cold`, `trials`, `return`. The tests require root only for the isolated `oblab-health-node` and `oblab-health-proxy` namespaces. Qdiscs exist only inside these namespaces. The proxy plus two 37.5 ms egress delays yields 150 ms path RTT. Each business direction sends a timestamped probe every 200 ms. OS ICMP echo is disabled inside the node namespace. `stop` closes/recreates a complete data-plane instance, not the host process or any system service.

`trials` drives 100 independent same-relay UDP trials against an existing TCP path, each with a three-second deadline, bypassing normal cold-scan spacing for test duration. It verifies the active connection identity, switch count, absence of data handshakes on the failed UDP path, p95 business RTT and longest reply gap. A separate state-machine test exercises 100 failed attempts. `return` uses real time; the ten-minute boundary-flapping test uses a simulated clock.

All network tests clean up their own namespaces. Windows/macOS are cross-vet/build coverage, not real-machine acceptance. The A3 result report distinguishes the initial 30-round matrix from the final five-round-per-mode regression, and cold results from hot standby results.
